AI has a strong foundation in mathematics.

Speaker script

There are three reasons we're taking AI's maths capability seriously. First, the training resources are large. NVIDIA's OpenMathReasoning release contains 3.2 million generated reasoning solutions across 306,000 distinct problems from maths forums, including high-school olympiad material. Those are training examples, not millions of independently verified curriculum questions.

Second, there's measured performance. DeepSeek-R1 reports 97.3 percent pass@1 on MATH-500. That's a 500-problem competition-maths evaluation, not a million-problem test. Part of R1's training rewards correct mathematical answers, so the model learns from a concrete correctness signal.

Third, these systems are contributing to research. In 2025, AlphaEvolve found a way to multiply two four-by-four complex matrices using 48 scalar multiplications rather than 49. That improved a record dating to Strassen's 1969 algorithm, after 56 years. It used a search system with automatic evaluation. That's a specific research advance, not a claim that a chatbot can solve any open proof.

Together, that's strong evidence of mathematical capability. It doesn't mean errors are impossible. These are different systems, and Mathify uses Claude. We still need to validate our generated questions and the tutor workflow. A few target-user sample cases and a short meeting with tutors would give us a much more relevant check. No tutor questionnaire responses have been received.

If asked about the benchmark: pass@1 is not success in any one of 64 attempts. The authors sample 64 responses per query to estimate pass@1 where sampling is required. The OpenMathReasoning authors also corrected their original 540,000-problem figure to 306,000 problems in the released solution set. We use the corrected number. We do not have live outputs and review records to substantiate a few-dozen-case 100 percent Mathify accuracy figure.

A second pass can catch mistakes the first missed.

Speaker script

The checker's job is simple: look for mistakes before we rely on the generated material. It gets the original problem and checks the breakdown, answer keys, options, explanations, hints and whether the retry is actually simpler. It flags issues; it doesn't automatically fix them.

There's research behind separating writing an answer from checking it. Weng and colleagues tested self-verification on maths word problems. With code-davinci-002, their GSM8K result went from 60.81 percent to 65.14 percent, a gain of 4.33 percentage points. They generated candidate answers, worked backwards to see whether those answers fit the problem's conditions, and used that check to select an answer. That is more specific than just asking the model to try again.

In machine-learning terms, it's generate-and-verify: give the checking stage its own task and criteria. Checking an answer against constraints can be easier than finding it from scratch. Comparing several candidates also uses extra computation at answer time rather than changing the model's training. Earlier work by Cobbe and colleagues trained verifiers to select among candidate solutions and also found a benefit.

Our checker follows the idea of a separate, criteria-based review, but it does not implement those papers' trained verifier or backward-selection method. We can't transfer their improvement figure to Mathify. It uses the same provider as generation, so both calls may share a blind spot. That's why we need to measure errors caught and good questions wrongly flagged, not just count how often it says everything is fine.

Repeated tests make failures visible.

Speaker script

This is where evaluation fits. A test harness is just a repeatable way to run the same problems, keep the outputs, and see what changed. It lets us measure both the generator and the checker instead of treating them as one black box.

We'd start with reviewed examples, run the generator and checker, inspect failures, change a prompt, then run the same tests again. We'd track correct answers, concept match, errors caught and false alarms. We'd also keep held-out examples for final evaluation, so we're not just tuning to the questions we've already seen.

There are tools for different parts of that work. LangSmith keeps traces of individual calls and compares runs. promptfoo repeats examples and checks expected outputs or rules. DeepEval organizes scoring against evaluation criteria. They're options, not three integrations we need at once, and they're not currently connected to Mathify. We already have a small local runner to build on.

We can start without tutor-labelled examples: check output structure, use problems with known answers, create deliberate mistakes and explore edge cases. Synthetic examples help us discover failures, but they don't independently validate real tutor use. Tutors don't block all technical testing; they are needed to judge whether our questions and workflow fit their work.

The useful ask is permission to pursue this, a small set of de-identified sample cases supplied with permission, and a short tutor meeting. Qualified reviewers would confirm the reference answers and probing pairs. We'd accept valid alternatives rather than exact wording. That would give us a manageable starting point, not a promise of effortless completion or guaranteed accuracy. These tools help us observe inputs, outputs and failures; they don't reveal every internal model calculation or improve the system automatically.
